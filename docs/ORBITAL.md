# All Switzerland: orbital experiment

Choose **Orbital view** beneath the main screen’s title, use the matching entry in the study browser, or open `?view=orbital` directly. This is a lazy-loaded public-transport view with national framing, camera orbit/pan/zoom, a shared full-day clock, 1–600× playback, a 0–10-minute trail control and measured rendering FPS. Reduced-motion users start with playback and camera drift paused. Dragging stops automatic camera drift.

From a geographic atlas map, entry makes a 1.7-second camera ascent, dissolving the atlas geometry into the orbital terrain partway through the flight. Both renderers receive the same geographically converted camera position, target and interpolated field of view. This is a geometric alignment and crossfade, not a morph between individual meshes. Departure waits for terrain and the first movement frame; cancellation, a load failure or a 45-second preparation timeout returns control to the atlas. The atlas stays mounted with its study, layer choices, selections, zoom and pan intact; timetable playback is suspended and its national-map render loop stops while orbit is active. The return link and browser Back descend from the current orbital camera to the saved atlas pose. A changed viewport aspect adjusts the return lens for phone rotation. Reduced motion skips the flight. Direct orbital links have no previous camera to restore and return to the default atlas; non-map studies retain their state with a dissolve. Large orbital resources are released on exit.

The combined view discovers all public full-day transport networks in `public/data` (`*-day.json` and `*-day-manifest.json`), including feeds not yet listed in the atlas selector. `STUDY_SOURCES` and the established Luzern, Zug and Thurgau names supply stable identifiers and duplicate precedence, and must remain available. Discovery chooses the newest weekday per feed, preferring a top-level release alias when its date matches a dated fixture. Older copies, Sunday alternatives and non-network datasets are recorded as exclusions rather than added as extra services. Local archival feeds are excluded; discovery never follows symlinks into them. In particular, Fribourg, Aargau and St. Gallen research feeds remain outside public orbital assets.

Every rebuild writes `data/orbital-source-audit.json`, showing each selected file, service date, manifest SHA-256, combined manifest/chunk SHA-256, raw journey count and retained count after cross-feed deduplication, plus every excluded candidate and its reason. A retained count of zero means all that feed's journey identities were represented by equal or longer journeys from other feeds. Source manifests must stay unchanged during the build; chunk windows, counts, and supplied byte/hash pins are checked before combining them. The September 9 build with the regional integration includes 26 public feeds, including Riviera, Kiental and Fribourg, with 177,046 distinct journey representations and a sampled peak of 5,344 simultaneous services. The tracked audit records this build. Counts change with regenerated source feeds.

A composite weekday disclosure remains visible; source dates, feed versions, source metadata and the inclusion list are retained in the generated manifest. It is not an observed single-date census or a count of tracked physical vehicles. Frequency instances remain representative departures.

`npm run data:orbital` regenerates `public/data/orbital/`; the explicit `npm run data:prepare` step also runs it before publishing a new R2 data release. Ordinary app builds reuse the pinned release without regenerating orbital blocks. Generated files are ignored by Git. Duplicate copies within chunks are removed. Across studies, source journey identifiers are normalized, preserving representative departure suffixes and preceding-service-day identity. The longest available journey wins, with stable source order breaking ties. This intentionally does not join inconsistent schedules across source dates or splice disconnected regional fragments.

Source stop arrivals and departures are retained. Paths are oriented from each departure stop and simplified at approximately 75 metres in the orbital projection. Short connectors to the source stops and unmatched stop-to-stop interpolation follow the source study's model. A perspective camera overlooks real swissALTIRegio relief on a geographical grid. The nationwide 769 × 449 grid has approximately 506 × 584 metre spacing and adds about 573 KiB compressed. The **Mountain detail** switch compares this standard layer with a 2305 × 1345 fine grid at approximately 169 × 195 metres: nine times as many samples, drawn from a finer source overview. The fine artifact adds about 4.73 MiB compressed and loads only when selected; both layers are then cached for comparison without changing the camera or clock. Elevations use the same 4.5× vertical exaggeration in both layers. Lakes have flat, DEM-derived levels (median interior grid-cell heights, lower shoreline samples for sub-grid lakes) and stylised specular moonlight. Their levels are not live water observations. Lights and trail samples are lifted onto the exact rendered terrain triangles; bridge decks and suspended cables are not modelled. Mapped underground railway passages are hidden as described below. The view does not claim globe curvature. `node scripts/ingest-orbital-terrain.mjs` regenerates the separately stored `public/data/orbital-terrain.json`; the source, checksum, sampling grid and lake levels are recorded in `data/orbital-terrain-audit.json`. Add `--detailed` to regenerate `public/data/orbital-terrain-detailed.json` and its corresponding audit. Normal builds use these saved artifacts without fetching elevation data.

Each two-hour movement file contains a Float32 header, journey table, time/x/z trajectory knots and tunnel entry/exit time pairs. Binary version 2 uses seven floats per journey (including tunnel-table offset and count); the decoder also accepts legacy version 1 blocks without tunnels. The stored files use gzip compression. Every descriptor pins byte lengths and SHA-256 for both compressed and decoded data. Fetch may automatically decompress HTTP responses with `Content-Encoding: gzip`; the loader detects the actual payload, decompresses only when needed, and always verifies the decoded checksum before validating table offsets and window identity. Unchanged gzip payloads also undergo compressed-byte verification. Each block includes journeys that ended within the preceding ten minutes so maximum-length trails survive transitions. The browser caches at most the current, preceding and next block. Movement blocks cross the React scene boundary through a stable getter, and only movement buffers remount when the block changes, keeping large typed arrays out of development-mode prop-diff performance records. Camera and terrain remain mounted. It pauses at a block edge, retaining the previous movements while the next block loads, and wraps at midnight. Scrubbing cancels superseded loads. Load failures have an explicit retry.

Rendering uses one dynamic point geometry for vehicle cores and halos, and one dynamic line geometry for fading trails. Only populated buffer ranges are uploaded. Services ease in and out inside their scheduled journey endpoints using a smooth envelope, approximately 1.2 real seconds at accelerated playback speeds (shortened for very brief journeys). Cores, halos and trail samples share the envelope. Rail, bus, tram, metro, cableway, funicular and boat each have an independent toggle, with smooth category fades; all may be deselected. The visible-service counter follows these filters, while the timeline profile still describes the whole day. Selected active services have no regional zoom culling; underground services remain in the active count while their lights are hidden. One-minute active-service totals form the timeline's day profile. The data builder and playback tests cover identity, frequency instances, direction, dwells, chunk boundaries and malformed buffers.

The default-off **Sunlight** control replaces the studio lighting with a sun direction calculated from [NOAA’s approximate solar-position equations](https://gml.noaa.gov/grad/solcalc/solareqns.PDF). Its explicit reference is 8 September 2026 in CEST (UTC+02:00), at 46.8° N, 8.23° E; browser time zones do not affect it. A parallel directional light follows the playback clock, with warm low-angle illumination, diffuse twilight and no direct sunlight below the geometric horizon. The astronomical direction is approximate; sky intensity and colour are illustrative, not radiometric weather simulation. Terrain and lakes receive a 1024-pixel shadow map while enabled, cast from a mesh capped at the standard grid density while the visible terrain retains its selected detail. Shadows use the displayed 4.5× exaggerated terrain; no atmospheric refraction, site-specific horizon correction or cloud model is applied. Switching it off restores the original lighting and disables shadow rendering. No external API calls or new data downloads are needed.

City and regional-hub names appear on pointer hover, using 81 anchors taken from the public national timetable’s station coordinates (station suffixes removed for display). A small screen-space hit area selects the nearest anchor; labels fade in and out over 280 ms, follow the camera and terrain, and stay hidden during dragging or when the pointer leaves the map. The HTML overlay accepts no pointer events. This is a curated orientation layer, not a municipal-boundary gazetteer, and it does not depend on selected transport types.

**Snow** enables simulated mountain snow cover, initially with a 2600 m snowline. The slider ranges from 800 to 4200 metres above sea level in 100 m steps; switching snow off restores the terrain palette. Coverage blends across a roughly 240 m transition, with stable spatial variation and less retention on steep slopes. Both altitude and slope compensate for the display’s vertical exaggeration. The material shader updates only uniforms when the slider changes, with eased transitions; no terrain rebuilds, added meshes or downloads are required. Lakes retain their water material, and snow responds to the selected scene lighting. This is illustrative cover, not measured snowpack, mapped glaciers or a forecast.

Railway lights and their trails fade out at mapped tunnel entrances and back in after exits. `scripts/ingest-orbital-tunnels.mjs` builds `public/data/orbital-tunnels.json` from a saved [Swiss Overpass](https://overpass.osm.ch/) extract (© [OpenStreetMap contributors](https://www.openstreetmap.org/copyright), ODbL). It supplements cross-border Simplon lines with the already pinned public OSM extract in `data/aargau-simplon-sources/`; it does not change that study or its reviewed routing policy. Source checksums, query and retrieval metadata are retained. Normal orbital builds use the saved geometry and never fetch tunnel data. The Swiss extract is current at retrieval, not an attic snapshot aligned to the composite timetable date.

`scripts/orbital-tunnels.mjs` matches rail, metro and funicular trajectory edges to mapped tunnel lines with a 100 m lateral tolerance and a direction cosine of at least 0.85. Road vehicles and cableways are not masked. Two reviewed coarse passages—Brig–Iselle (Simplon) and the Gotthard summit—also project mapped portal positions onto long timetable chords that pass within 1.5 km of both portals, with direction cosine at least 0.98 and bounded length. These account for existing stop-to-stop or very coarse geometry without replacing it. They are approximate visual visibility masks, not surveyed portal timing or underground elevations. Other unmatched or poorly aligned tunnel sections can remain visible; no elevation-only heuristic hides mountain railways. The generated `data/orbital-tunnel-audit.json` records matched ways, coarse passages and affected journey counts.

Tunnel intervals retain full journey timing across chunk boundaries. Opacity is zero throughout each interval, with smoothstep fades on the adjacent surface sections (1.2 seconds at ordinary playback speeds, capped at 60 timetable seconds). Historical trails are split at every portal, even when one trail sample would otherwise span a whole tunnel. Counts and schedules retain underground services. Tunnel lookup uses binary search and caller-owned buffers, with no additional meshes or per-frame allocation. Tests cover portals in both directions, cross-track and non-rail exclusions, coarse Simplon matching, two tunnels inside one trail chord, block-edge intervals, opacity and malformed binary tables.

The camera-altitude readout below the FPS updates while orbiting, panning and zooming, even with timetable playback paused. It reports approximate kilometres above the sea-level plane, using camera Y divided by the same vertical scale as the terrain (including removal of the 4.5× exaggeration). It is not slant distance to the orbit target or clearance above the terrain beneath the camera. This is a flat-map equivalent, not an Earth-curvature calculation. The text updates at most ten times per second through a DOM ref without rerendering the scene or announcing every change to screen readers.

**Focus view** (or **F**) fades away the header, readouts, settings, timeline and city hover labels. Playback and camera interaction continue with the existing settings and scene intact. A discreet **Show controls** button remains at the bottom right; **Escape** or **F** also restores the interface. Hidden controls are inert and removed from the accessibility tree, keyboard focus moves to the exit button and returns to the focus toggle on exit, and reduced-motion preferences disable the fade. Loading notices are hidden in focus mode; actionable load errors remain available.

On narrow screens (up to 700 CSS pixels), and compact landscape screens (up to 1000 × 500), the default chrome is a small header and a thumb-sized playback bar with the clock, play/pause, Focus and Controls. The native Controls dialog holds transport filters, playback speed, trails, snow, lighting, terrain detail and the remaining view actions. It scrolls independently, traps keyboard focus and returns focus on dismissal. Portrait uses a bottom sheet; landscape uses a bounded side sheet. Rotation adjusts framing while preserving the camera angle and panned location, without remounting the scene; returning to a desktop layout dismisses the sheet. All phone buttons, selects and slider tracks have at least 44 CSS pixels of height, inputs retain readable sizes, and `dvh` plus safe-area insets keep the interface within the usable screen. Browser page zoom is enabled; map gestures remain scoped to the canvas.

Touch controls explicitly map one finger to orbit and two fingers to combined pinch-zoom/pan. On coarse pointers, panning stays in the ground plane. A short city tap uses a 32-pixel selection radius and holds the name for 2.5 seconds; the label remains attached to that city while the camera moves. Drags, two-finger gestures, releases outside the canvas and cancelled pointers do not produce city taps. Desktop hover retains its 24-pixel radius. Gesture-sequence tests cover taps, coalesced moves, both multi-touch release orders, cancellation and mouse hover. Responsive browser checks cover 320 × 568, 390 × 844, 430 × 932 and 844 × 390 layouts; these are viewport checks, not a physical-device Safari or thermal-performance benchmark.
